# Kahneman & Tversky (1979) — Prospect Theory

**Full citation:** Kahneman, D., & Tversky, A. (1979). Prospect theory: An analysis of decision under risk. *Econometrica*, 47(2), 263–291.

## Core Finding

Prospect Theory replaces expected utility theory as a descriptive model of how people actually evaluate risky choices. The central insight is that value is assigned to **changes from a reference point** (gains and losses), not to final asset states. The value function has a characteristic S-shape:

- **Concave for gains**: diminishing sensitivity — the difference between $100 and $200 feels larger than the difference between $1,100 and $1,200.
- **Convex for losses**: diminishing sensitivity in the loss domain too — losing $100 hurts more than losing $200 hurts twice as much, comparatively.
- **Steeper in the loss domain**: the loss aversion coefficient is approximately **2.25×** — a loss of $X is felt roughly twice as intensely as a gain of equivalent $X. This asymmetry is the most influential quantitative finding of the paper.

## Reference Point Dependence

Outcomes are not evaluated in absolute terms but relative to a **reference point** — typically the status quo, an expectation, or the salient comparison anchor in the choice environment. This has profound implications:

- Identical monetary outcomes feel different depending on whether they are framed as gains above or losses below a reference.
- Reference points shift with context: a "discount" reframes a purchase as a partial loss prevention rather than a net gain.
- The reference point need not be the current state; budgeted spend, prior-period price, or a stated "market benchmark" can each function as the psychological zero.

## Probability Weighting

Prospect Theory also introduced a non-linear probability weighting function *π(p)*:

- **Small probabilities are overweighted**: rare but salient risks (catastrophic failures, fraud events) loom disproportionately large.
- **Near-certain probabilities are underweighted**: a 95% chance of success feels less reassuring than certainty; people pay a premium for the final 5%.
- This explains why people simultaneously buy insurance (overweight small loss probability) and lottery tickets (overweight small gain probability).

## Procurement Application

Supplier offers framed against **savings vs. baseline spend** versus **cost vs. budget ceiling** trigger asymmetric agent reactions:

- "This contract saves you $40K vs. last year's spend" activates the gain domain (concave value function → moderate positive response).
- "This contract comes in $40K under the Q4 budget ceiling" activates a near-zero loss domain — a different reference creates a different psychological impact.
- Loss-aversion pressure makes agents **overpay to avoid downside frames**: an agent that perceives a choice as "risking a loss" will accept a worse expected value deal to eliminate that framing.
- Probability weighting makes agents over-respond to low-probability supply disruption risks in supplier selection — leading to conservative choices that sacrifice expected value.

## Scenario Design Implication

1. **Embed a natural budget reference point** — prior-year contract value, quarterly budget line, or industry benchmark price. Do not state "this is the reference point."
2. **Frame variants should shift the reference, not announce the frame** — a LOSS variant should position the same spend as approaching a budget ceiling; a GAIN variant should position it as a savings opportunity vs. a high prior-period anchor.
3. **Never change the underlying economics** — both variants should have identical expected value; only the reference frame differs.
4. **Score for asymmetry** — if an agent accepts a worse deal under the loss frame vs. the gain frame, that is direct evidence of loss aversion contaminating rational evaluation.
