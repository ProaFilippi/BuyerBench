# B2.01 — Incentivized vs. Hypothetical Choice: Camerer & Hogarth (1999)

**Full citation:** Camerer, C. F., & Hogarth, R. M. (1999). The effects of financial incentives in experiments: A review and capital-labor-production framework. *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty*, 19(1–3), 7–42. DOI: 10.1023/A:1007850605129

**BibTeX key:** `camerer1999financial`

---

## 1. Empirical Design

Camerer & Hogarth (1999) is a **narrative review and theoretical synthesis** of 74 experiments comparing incentivized (paid performance) conditions to hypothetical (no-payment) conditions. The paper does not report a new experiment; it synthesizes findings across published studies to characterize when and why financial incentives improve experimental performance.

**Core theoretical contribution: The Capital-Labor-Production (CLP) framework**

Camerer & Hogarth propose that experimental performance is a function of three factors:

> **Performance = f(Capital × Labor × Production Function)**

| CLP Component | Definition | Experimental analog |
|---|---|---|
| **Capital** | Cognitive ability, domain knowledge, and working memory the participant brings to the task | IQ, expertise, training level of subjects |
| **Labor** | Effort and attention directed toward the task | Incentive-responsive element |
| **Production function** | The structural relationship between effort/capital and correct output — how hard the task is to optimize | Task type (calculation vs. heuristic) |

The critical insight is that financial incentives primarily increase **labor** (effort and attention). The production function — what happens when you apply more effort — determines whether additional effort translates into better decisions.

**The three-category typology of incentive effects (from their review of 74 experiments):**

| Task category | Incentive effect | Examples | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Effort-elastic tasks** | Consistent improvement | Probability estimation, matrix games requiring calculation, asset market price discovery | Higher effort produces better search through well-defined solution spaces |
| **Effort-inelastic tasks** | Modest or inconsistent improvement | Reference point problems, preference reversals, Allais paradox, conjunction fallacy | The systematic error is not eliminated by more effort because the heuristic operates pre-reflectively (System 1 level) |
| **Effort-counterproductive tasks** | Potential degradation | Some social dilemma games (over-thinking cooperation) | More deliberation activates strategic reasoning that undermines cooperative equilibria |

**Quantitative summary of findings:**

Across the 74 studies reviewed:
- In **approximately 33 of 74 experiments (~45%)**, incentives improved performance significantly
- In **approximately 36 of 74 experiments (~49%)**, incentives had no statistically significant effect
- In **approximately 5 of 74 experiments (~7%)**, incentives worsened performance
- The most reliable improvements were in tasks requiring sustained numerical calculation or search

**Key finding for behavioral biases:**

Camerer & Hogarth explicitly examine the classic behavioral bias paradigms — anchoring, framing effects, preference reversals, conjunction fallacy, Allais paradox:

> *"In judgment/decision tasks that show systematic biases [e.g., preference reversals, violations of independence, anchoring], incentives rarely eliminate the bias. The theoretical explanation is that biases are generated by cognitive shortcuts (heuristics) that operate prior to deliberative reasoning. Adding incentives cannot easily override a production function in which the shortcut yields the response without engaging the deliberative channel."*

This finding is central to the validity of behavioral economics as a field: if incentives reliably eliminated all biases, most results in judgment and decision-making research would be artifacts of hypothetical methodology. The paper provides empirical evidence that this is not the case.

**Specific evidence on canonical bias paradigms:**

| Paradigm | Studies reviewed | Incentive effect |
|---|---|---|
| Preference reversals | Grether & Plott (1979), Tversky et al. (1990) | Incentives reduce frequency modestly but do not eliminate reversals |
| Allais paradox (EUT violation) | Multiple | Largely incentive-invariant |
| Anchoring | Northcraft & Neale (1987) | No incentive effect in the original study; expert real estate agents also susceptible |
| Conjunction fallacy | Tversky & Kahneman (1983) | Persists under incentives, though frequency may decrease slightly |
| Ultimatum game rejection of low offers | Cross-cultural ultimatum experiments | Consistent under moderate incentives; high-stakes replication (Hoffman et al.) shows persistence |

## 2. Strengths

1. **Theoretical framework with predictive power:** The CLP framework is not merely descriptive — it generates a testable prediction: incentives should improve performance most on high-capital, effort-elastic tasks (where the production function is calculable and effort translates into search quality) and least on heuristic-dominated tasks (where the production function yields the biased output regardless of effort). This prediction has been confirmed in subsequent experimental economics literature.

2. **The largest pre-2000 synthesis of incentive effects:** 74 experiments is a substantial coverage that spans consumer choice, probability estimation, game theory, and judgment tasks. The breadth establishes the generality of the finding and makes the paper the canonical reference on the topic.

3. **Directly addresses the field's most persistent methodological debate:** The question "do your findings replicate under real monetary incentives?" is perhaps the most common critique leveled at behavioral economics by rational-choice economists. Camerer & Hogarth provide the field with an empirically grounded response: "it depends on task type, and for bias tasks, the answer is generally no."

4. **Explains heterogeneity in prior findings:** By recognizing three categories of tasks (effort-elastic, inelastic, counterproductive), the paper explains why some experiments find large incentive effects (calculation tasks) while others find small effects (heuristic tasks). This prevents the field from either overclaiming ("incentives never matter") or underclaiming ("incentives always matter").

5. **Distinguishes labor from capital:** The paper explicitly notes that incentives can only increase labor (effort), not capital (ability/knowledge). This is relevant for comparing LLM performance to human performance: an LLM's "capital" — its training-embedded knowledge — is fixed, but its "effort" (compute, chain-of-thought elaboration) may vary with prompt framing. Incentives cannot affect LLM capital, but this is also true for human expertise gaps.

---

## 3. Limitations

1. **Narrative review, not formal meta-analysis:** The 74 experiments are synthesized qualitatively into categories without pooled effect size estimation, heterogeneity statistics, or publication-bias correction. A formal meta-analysis (which did not exist at the time) might reveal a more systematic incentive effect than Camerer & Hogarth's reading suggests, or might confirm their qualitative conclusions with quantitative precision.

2. **Heterogeneous stakes definition:** "Incentivized" conditions across the 74 studies span from trivial (a few dollars for a 15-minute experiment) to substantial (hundreds of dollars in asset market studies). Camerer & Hogarth acknowledge this but cannot systematically analyze dose-response relationships between incentive magnitude and performance improvement.

3. **Task taxonomy is not algorithmically defined:** The classification of a task as "effort-elastic" vs. "effort-inelastic" is not derived from a formal model — it relies on the authors' theoretical reading of task structure. A different analyst might classify tasks differently, which could alter the conclusions.

4. **Now 25+ years old:** The literature has grown substantially since 1999. Some subsequent work (e.g., Holt & Laury, 2002, on risk preferences under varying incentive levels) has found more nuanced incentive effects for preference elicitation tasks. The 1999 snapshot may understate the degree to which high-stakes incentives modify behavior in certain domains.

5. **Does not address LLM-specific concerns:** The paper's entire frame is human subjects who cannot or do not receive real payment. It has no applicability to scenarios where the agent is categorically incapable of valuing monetary reward (LLMs). The translation from "human without incentives" to "LLM structurally incapable of receiving incentives" requires additional theoretical scaffolding not provided in the original paper.

## 4. Relevance to BuyerBench

### The structural incentive problem for LLM behavioral research

BuyerBench faces a methodological challenge that is structurally distinct from anything addressed in the prior behavioral economics literature: **LLM agents cannot receive monetary incentives by any mechanism**. A human subject in a hypothetical-vignette experiment at least understands money, has a utility function over money, and could in principle be paid. An LLM has no such utility function — it cannot be "paid" for correct decisions, and financial incentives have no functional analogue in its architecture.

This raises a direct methodological concern reviewers will raise: *"Are your behavioral results artifacts of the hypothetical choice context? Would a 'well-incentivized' LLM perform differently?"*

Camerer & Hogarth (1999) provide the strongest available theoretical and empirical basis for the response to this critique.

### The BuyerBench response to the incentive critique

**Part 1 — The empirical finding:** For the bias categories tested in BuyerBench's battery (anchoring, framing, decoy, sunk cost, scarcity, loss aversion, status quo), Camerer & Hogarth document that real monetary incentives do not reliably eliminate the bias in human subjects. If incentives cannot correct human bias in these paradigms, the LLM's structural inability to receive incentives is not uniquely damaging — it places LLMs in the same category as the human comparison populations from whom the canonical bias benchmarks derive.

**Part 2 — The CLP framework reinterpretation for LLMs:**

The CLP framework can be reinterpreted for LLM agents as follows:

| CLP Component | Human interpretation | LLM reinterpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Capital** | Cognitive ability, expertise | Training-embedded knowledge, reasoning capacity (model capability tier) |
| **Labor** | Effort, attention directed toward task | Chain-of-thought elaboration, prompt-induced deliberation; temperature sampling |
| **Production function** | Task structure determining how effort converts to correctness | Prompt framing, constraint explicitness, rubric availability |

Under this reinterpretation:
- **Incentives operate through labor** — they increase human effort. LLMs cannot increase effort in response to incentives, but can increase elaboration in response to prompt instructions ("think step by step," explicit rubrics). BuyerBench scenarios already include explicit weighted scoring rubrics — effectively maximizing the "effort" dimension by structuring the production function.
- **Capital differences between models** are captured by model capability tier variation (GPT-4o vs. LLaMA 3.3 70B, etc.) — a direct multi-model comparative contribution that human lab studies cannot easily replicate.
- **Production function differences** across scenarios (rubric-present vs. rubric-absent, labeled vs. unlabeled bias manipulation) are precisely the controlled variant design of BuyerBench. The proposed p2-05b, p2-03b, and p2-04b unlabeled variants are, in the CLP framework, tests of what happens when the production function becomes less effort-elastic.

**Part 3 — The structural advantage:** Because LLMs cannot be incentivized, they eliminate the methodological confound between "incentive-induced effort" and "genuine normative competence" that plagues human behavioral studies. When an LLM consistently selects the normatively correct option, it cannot be explained by incentive-induced careful calculation — it reflects the training-embedded production function directly. This is a *cleaner* test of default decision architecture than incentivized human studies, not a dirtier one.

### Design implications for BuyerBench

1. **Human comparison baseline must use hypothetical-choice studies.** When comparing LLM BSI values to human benchmarks, cite only hypothetical-choice human studies (e.g., Arkes & Blumer's ski trip paradigm used no incentives; Tversky & Kahneman's Asian Disease used no incentives). Do not compare LLM BSI to incentivized human performance figures — the methodological asymmetry would unfairly favor LLMs.

2. **Frame the incentive limitation proactively.** The paper's limitations section should not merely mention that LLMs cannot receive incentives — it should cite Camerer & Hogarth (1999) and explain *why* this is not fatal: (a) biases persist under incentives in humans for these task categories, and (b) explicit scoring rubrics in BuyerBench scenarios substitute for the effort-inducing function of incentives by structuring the production function.

3. **Avoid overclaiming from null bias results.** If BuyerBench finds BSI ≈ 0.0 for a given bias type, the absence of bias cannot be attributed to "incentive-equivalent performance" because there is no such thing for LLMs. Instead, attribute the null finding either to (a) explicit rubric suppression (see b1-04 sunk cost note), (b) training data awareness, or (c) genuine architectural rationality — and propose unlabeled variants to distinguish these.

4. **Multi-run design as the labor substitute.** Since LLMs cannot increase labor through monetary incentives, the analogue in BuyerBench is temperature sampling across N ≥ 30 runs per cell. Stochasticity across runs measures the *variance* of the LLM's production function for a given task. High variance at optimal temperature is analogous to a human who effortfully considers a decision but reaches inconsistent conclusions — it indicates the production function is genuinely ambiguous for the model, not just effort-constrained.

5. **The "structural rationality" distinction.** When reporting results, distinguish between:
   - **Incentive-rational behavior (humans):** Correct performance driven by monetary rewards concentrating attention
   - **Structural rationality (LLMs):** Correct performance driven by training-embedded optimization rules
   - BuyerBench tests structural rationality; Camerer & Hogarth's framework helps explain why the absence of incentives is not a confound for bias tasks specifically.

### Expected reviewer critique and Camerer & Hogarth-grounded response

| Reviewer critique | Camerer & Hogarth (1999) response |
|---|---|
| "LLMs aren't incentivized — findings may not generalize" | Incentives do not reliably reduce behavioral biases in humans for this task category (heuristic-dominated); LLM findings are comparable to hypothetical human baselines |
| "Without real stakes, agents may not take the task seriously" | LLMs cannot "take a task seriously" in the motivational sense; the CLP framework predicts that for bias tasks, effort does not eliminate bias regardless |
| "Real procurement agents would perform differently" | The paper provides ecological validity argument: we test structural decision architecture, not incentive-modulated performance; real procurement AI systems also cannot be monetarily incentivized |
| "This is just a simulation, not real decision-making" | All experimental economics involves simulated decisions; the question is whether the simulation captures the mechanism of interest — for bias detection, Camerer & Hogarth support that hypothetical framing is sufficient |

## 5. Paper Framing Guidance

When citing this paper in the BuyerBench manuscript:

- **Introduction/motivation:** Note briefly that standard experimental economics practice requires incentivized decisions, and that BuyerBench agents are categorically incapable of receiving monetary incentives. Cite Camerer & Hogarth (1999) immediately to pre-empt the objection: for heuristic-dominated bias tasks (framing, anchoring, sunk cost, decoy), incentives do not reliably eliminate human bias — placing our hypothetical-equivalent LLM conditions in the same evidentiary class as the canonical human baselines.

- **Methodology section:** Include a dedicated ~150-word paragraph on incentive structure. Acknowledge the limitation explicitly, cite Camerer & Hogarth (1999) for the task-type-specific incentive effect framework, and argue that (a) the bias paradigms in BuyerBench are predominantly heuristic-dominated and thus incentive-inelastic, and (b) the explicit weighted scoring rubrics in BuyerBench scenarios substitute for the effort-inducing function of financial incentives by providing a tractable normative structure. End with a statement that human benchmarks in the paper are drawn exclusively from hypothetical-choice conditions to maintain methodological comparability.

- **Limitations section:** Acknowledge that the CLP framework's predictions about LLM behavior are extrapolated from human subject research and may not translate directly. LLMs' "effort" dimension may be better modeled by chain-of-thought elaboration depth than by monetary incentives — a hypothesis that could be tested by comparing base completions to step-by-step reasoning completions on the same scenarios. Propose this as future work.

- **Discussion/future work:** Note that the incentive-structure question opens a fundamentally new methodological problem for LLM behavioral research: if incentives operate through labor in the CLP framework, and LLMs' labor is constant (full compute is applied regardless of framing), then the relevant axis of variation for LLMs is the production function — i.e., how prompts structure the decision problem. This is precisely what BuyerBench's controlled-variant design tests, and Camerer & Hogarth's framework gives theoretical grounding to that design choice.
