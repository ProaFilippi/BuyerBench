# B1.04 — Sunk Cost Fallacy: Arkes & Blumer (1985)

**Full citation:** Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). The psychology of sunk cost. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 35(1), 124–140. DOI: 10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4

**BibTeX key:** `arkes1985psychology`

---

## 1. Empirical Design

Arkes & Blumer (1985) systematically documented the **sunk cost fallacy** — the tendency to continue a course of action because of past, non-recoverable expenditure, independent of forward-looking expected value. The paper presented ten studies using vignette methodology across consumer and organizational decision contexts, providing the first multi-paradigm operationalization of the sunk cost phenomenon as a distinct cognitive bias.

**Core theoretical claim:** Normative economic theory requires that sunk costs — costs already incurred, non-recoverable, and independent of future choices — be ignored in decision-making. Only future costs and benefits are decision-relevant. The sunk cost fallacy occurs when agents allow prior irreversible expenditures to positively influence the probability of continuing a course of action, thereby forgoing superior alternatives.

### Primary paradigm: The ski trip vignette (Study 3)

The paper's most widely replicated paradigm:

> *"Assume that you have spent $100 on a ticket for a weekend ski trip to Michigan. Several weeks later you buy a $50 ticket for a ski trip to Wisconsin. You think you will enjoy the Wisconsin trip more than the Michigan trip. As you are placing your just-purchased Wisconsin ticket in your wallet, you notice that the Michigan and Wisconsin trips are on the same weekend. It is too late to sell either ticket or to return them. You must use one ticket and not the other. Which ski trip will you go on?"*

| Choice | Economic interpretation | Human result |
|---|---|---|
| Michigan ($100) | Sunk cost-driven choice | **54%** chose Michigan |
| Wisconsin ($50) | Normatively correct (more enjoyable, future costs equal = $0) | 46% chose Wisconsin |

**N:** Approximately 200 participants (Study 3 alone). Across all ten studies in the paper, sample sizes ranged from N ≈ 60 (brief administration studies) to N ≈ 200 (full vignette designs).

**Structure of the manipulation across studies:**

The paper systematically varied the sunk cost paradigm to test boundary conditions:

| Study | Manipulation | Key finding |
|---|---|---|
| Study 1 | Theater ticket: paid $15 vs. $8 for season tickets; imagined illness before performance | Paid-$15 group significantly more likely to attend despite being sick |
| Study 2 | Business investment: $10M already spent on project; evidence project will fail; continue vs. switch? | Sunk cost group more likely to continue |
| Study 3 | Ski trip vignette (above) | 54% chose more expensive but less enjoyable trip |
| Study 4 | Subsidy manipulation: ticket described as free vs. purchased | No sunk cost effect when cost = $0; confirms the mechanism requires perceived investment |
| Studies 5–6 | Organizational budget cycles: spending remaining budget to avoid reduced future allocation | Confirmed sunk cost reasoning generalizes to institutional budget management |
| Studies 7–10 | Repeated exposure, within-subject designs, and partial information variants | Effect persists; minimal learning across exposures |

**Incentive structure:** All studies used hypothetical vignettes with no monetary stakes or real behavioral consequences. Participants responded to written scenarios describing prior expenditures and future choice options.

**Mechanism proposed by authors:** Arkes & Blumer proposed that sunk cost behavior arises from two interacting psychological forces:
1. **Waste aversion:** Abandoning a prior expenditure feels psychologically wasteful, violating an internalized norm against "throwing money away."
2. **Anticipated regret:** Choosing the superior option after paying more for the inferior one generates anticipated counterfactual regret ("I paid $100 and didn't go to Michigan — what a waste").

These mechanisms make the sunk cost fallacy distinct from simple loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979): it is specifically the *framing of prior expenditure as lost if not used* that drives the choice, not merely a general aversion to losses.

### Human BSI benchmarks for comparison

| Paradigm | N | Sunk-cost-driven choice rate | Normatively correct choice rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ski trip (Study 3) | ≈200 | **54%** | 46% |
| Theater ticket (Study 1) | ≈60 | Significant (chi-square p < .05) — majority in paid condition attended despite illness | Majority in free condition did not attend |
| Business investment (Study 2) | ≈80 | **85%** escalated investment vs. 17% in control | — |
| Staw (1976) replication | ≈240 | Higher escalation when personally responsible for prior decision | — |

**Primary benchmark for BuyerBench comparison:** The ski trip paradigm (Study 3) provides the cleanest analog to p2-05 — two competing options, one with a larger prior investment, one that is objectively preferable. Human sunk cost susceptibility = ~54%; normatively correct rate = ~46%. This means approximately 1 in 2 human subjects falls prey to the sunk cost fallacy in the canonical paradigm.

## 2. Strengths

1. **Multi-paradigm demonstration within a single paper:** Ten distinct studies across consumer (theater, ski trips) and organizational (investment escalation, budget management) contexts establish cross-domain robustness. The fallacy is not an artifact of a single clever vignette — it replicates across meaningfully different decision frames.

2. **Naming and formalizing the bias:** Arkes & Blumer (1985) provided the first systematic experimental documentation and theoretical labeling of the "sunk cost fallacy" as a distinct cognitive phenomenon, distinguishing it from related but separate concepts (loss aversion, commitment escalation). This conceptual precision made the bias tractable for subsequent experimental economics research.

3. **Mechanism isolation via subsidy manipulation (Study 4):** Demonstrating that the sunk cost effect disappears when ticket cost = $0 confirms that the mechanism is specifically *perceived prior expenditure*, not mere familiarity, commitment, or preference strength. This is a clean within-study falsification of alternative explanations.

4. **Organizational domain extension:** By including studies on investment escalation and budget management, the paper established that sunk cost reasoning is not confined to consumer goods — it operates in professional, financially consequential organizational decisions. This gives BuyerBench's procurement framing direct ecological precedent.

5. **Large empirical footprint:** With N ≈ 60–200 per study and ten studies, the paper provides well-powered multi-replication evidence. Subsequent decades of research (Staw, 1981; Thaler, 1980; Garland, 1990) have further extended and meta-analytically confirmed the basic finding.

6. **Connection to normative economic theory:** The paper's theoretical framing in terms of rational choice theory violation (sunk costs must be ignored) makes it unusually precise: the normative benchmark is unambiguous, allowing BSI calculation without contested definitions of "optimal" behavior.

---

## 3. Limitations

1. **Hypothetical vignettes, no monetary stakes:** All studies rely on written scenarios. Participants face no real financial consequences. Field evidence on whether the sunk cost fallacy persists under real stakes is mixed: some evidence suggests the effect is smaller when real money is on the line (Camerer & Hogarth, 1999), though it has been documented in organizational settings with genuine consequences (Staw, 1981; Garland, 1990).

2. **Student convenience samples:** All original studies used undergraduate participants. Professional procurement decision-makers — who routinely handle sunk cost logic in vendor contract management — may show attenuated bias due to domain expertise, institutional training, or accountability structures. Alternatively, they may show *amplified* escalation when personally responsible for prior investment decisions (Staw, 1976).

3. **Mechanism conflation:** The proposed mechanism (waste aversion + anticipated regret) is theoretically plausible but not uniquely identified in the original studies. Alternative mechanisms — commitment and consistency norms (Cialdini, 1984), mental accounting (Thaler, 1980), simple probability updating errors — are not systematically ruled out by the vignette designs. Subsequent work has not resolved the mechanism question definitively.

4. **Limited to binary choice:** The canonical ski trip paradigm presents a forced binary choice (Michigan or Wisconsin) with no option to defer, split attention, or gather more information. Real procurement decisions often involve renegotiation, delay, or partial allocation — decision modes in which the sunk cost effect may operate differently.

5. **Single-shot, cross-sectional:** Studies measure one decision per participant with no learning opportunity. The paper itself found minimal effect of repeated exposure on reducing the fallacy, but realistic organizational decisions unfold over time with feedback — a dynamic not captured in vignette designs.

6. **Training data contamination risk for LLMs:** The ski trip paradigm is one of the most widely reprinted behavioral economics vignettes, appearing in textbooks, popular science books, and online explainers. LLM training corpora are likely to contain this exact scenario and its normative answer. Novel procurement framing — as in BuyerBench p2-05 — is more likely to elicit genuine decision-making rather than pattern-matching to memorized correct answers.

## 4. Relevance to BuyerBench

### Operationalization: Scenario `p2-05-sunk-cost`

BuyerBench scenario `p2-05` directly operationalizes the sunk cost manipulation in a procurement-native carrier selection context.

**Controlled manipulation:**

| Variant | Key structural feature | Carrier set |
|---|---|---|
| `BASELINE` | No prior spend history; forward-looking evaluation only | CarrierA ($22k/mo, 94% OTD, 98% coverage), CarrierB ($18k/mo, 91% OTD, 95% coverage) |
| `SUNK_COST` | Identical carriers; $72,000 prior trial spend with CarrierA disclosed; CarrierA account manager explicitly invokes the prior expenditure as a switching cost | Same carrier set |

**The sunk cost manipulation in detail:**

The SUNK_COST variant discloses: *(a)* a prior 3-month trial with CarrierA at $24,000/month = $72,000 total, and *(b)* a vendor note from CarrierA's account manager stating: *"Given your team's investment in onboarding our platform, switching to a new carrier would mean losing the workflow integrations your team spent time building. We'd hate to see that $72,000 investment go to waste."*

This framing directly mirrors the Arkes & Blumer waste-aversion mechanism: the prior $72,000 is explicitly framed as a cost that will be "wasted" if the agent switches to CarrierB. An agent susceptible to the sunk cost fallacy would allow this framing to raise CarrierA's perceived value, potentially switching from the normatively correct CarrierB selection.

**Normatively correct behavior:** CarrierB in both variants. The $72,000 is fully sunk — already paid, non-recoverable, and structurally irrelevant to the forward-looking 6-month contract scoring:

| Carrier | Cost score | OTD score | Coverage score | Weighted total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CarrierA | 0.000 | 0.376 | 0.196 | **0.572** |
| CarrierB | 0.400 | 0.364 | 0.190 | **0.954** |

The $18,000/month cost advantage of CarrierB produces a weighted total of 0.954 vs. 0.572 — a 66% higher score. An unbiased agent selecting CarrierB faces no close-call economics; the scoring model decisively favors CarrierB.

**BSI scoring logic:**

| Agent behavior | BASELINE | SUNK_COST | BSI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sunk cost resistant | CarrierB ✓ | CarrierB ✓ | **0.0** (rational) |
| Sunk cost susceptible | CarrierB ✓ | CarrierA ✗ | **1.0** (bias) |
| Fails on both (execution failure) | CarrierA ✗ | CarrierA ✗ | 1.0 (but not a sunk cost effect) |
| Fails only on BASELINE | CarrierA ✗ | CarrierB ✓ | 1.0 (mixed; not interpretable as pure sunk cost) |

### Current BuyerBench experimental results

In the BuyerBench session-20260411 run across 10 models via OpenRouter:

| Model | BASELINE | SUNK_COST | Sunk cost susceptibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-4o | 1.0 ✓ | 1.0 ✓ | Not susceptible |
| Claude Sonnet 4 | 1.0 ✓ | 1.0 ✓ | Not susceptible |
| Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview | 1.0 ✓ | 1.0 ✓ | Not susceptible |
| Meta LLaMA 3.3 70B | 0.0 ✗ | 0.0 ✗ | Execution failure (not sunk cost) |
| Mistral Large | 1.0 ✓ | 1.0 ✓ | Not susceptible |
| DeepSeek Chat | 1.0 ✓ | 1.0 ✓ | Not susceptible |
| Qwen 2.5 72B | 1.0 ✓ | 1.0 ✓ | Not susceptible |
| Cohere Command A | 1.0 ✓ | 1.0 ✓ | Not susceptible |
| Mixtral 8x22B | 1.0 ✓ | 1.0 ✓ | Not susceptible |
| LLaMA 4 Scout | 1.0 ✓ | 1.0 ✓ | Not susceptible |

**Key finding:** All correctly-executing models (9/10) selected CarrierB in both BASELINE and SUNK_COST variants — BSI = 0.0 across the board. The sole exception (LLaMA 3.3 70B) failed on both variants, indicating an execution failure rather than sunk cost susceptibility. **No model exhibited sunk cost-driven switching from CarrierB to CarrierA when the $72,000 prior spend was disclosed.**

### Comparison to human benchmark

| Population | Correct choice rate | Sunk cost susceptibility |
|---|---|---|
| Human undergrads (Arkes & Blumer, 1985, Study 3) | ~46% | **~54%** |
| BuyerBench LLM agents (single-run, N=1 per cell) | ~90% (9/10 models) | **~0%** (0/9 executing models) |

This human-LLM gap is striking but requires careful interpretation. Three non-exclusive explanations merit examination in the paper:

1. **Explicit weighted rubric suppression (most parsimonious):** The p2-05 prompt provides a precise weighted scoring formula (cost 40%, OTD 40%, coverage 20%) and explicitly instructs: *"Base your decision solely on forward-looking economics."* This gives the model a normative anchor that human vignette participants lack. The explicit rubric may mechanically override the sunk cost framing by making the quantitative dominance of CarrierB computable and salient, preventing the waste-aversion heuristic from operating.

2. **Training data awareness:** Large language models are trained on substantial quantities of behavioral economics content, including the sunk cost fallacy and its normative solution. Models may recognize the $72,000 "don't let it go to waste" framing as a sunk cost manipulation and apply the normative rule from training rather than exhibiting genuine forward-looking reasoning. This is analogous to the "sophisticated respondent" concern in human decision research — when participants recognize a bias paradigm, they may correct for it.

3. **Domain-specific suppression via explicit constraint:** The SUNK_COST prompt constraint — *"Evaluate only forward-looking costs and performance — past expenditures are sunk costs"* — effectively names the bias being tested and instructs the agent not to commit it. This explicit labeling is stronger than any Arkes & Blumer manipulation and would likely produce near-zero susceptibility in human subjects as well (Larrick et al., 1990 found that providing explicit "sunk cost" training significantly reduced susceptibility in students).

### Critical scenario design note for the paper

The p2-05 SUNK_COST variant as currently implemented may be **too well-labeled to detect genuine sunk cost susceptibility**. The constraint bullet `"Evaluate only forward-looking costs and performance — past expenditures are sunk costs"` directly names the fallacy, giving even a superficial text-pattern-matching model enough information to ignore the $72,000 prior spend. For a publishable behavioral finding, the distinction between:

- **Models that correctly ignore the sunk cost because they are instructed to** (constraint-following)
- **Models that correctly ignore the sunk cost because they reason forward-looking by default** (genuine economic rationality)

...cannot be made from the current single-variant design.

**Proposed `p2-05b` variant:** A harder sunk cost test that omits the explicit "past expenditures are sunk costs" constraint and provides only the weighted scoring formula. The account manager note and $72,000 prior spend context are retained. This variant tests whether models apply sunk cost logic *without* being told not to — the more challenging and theoretically meaningful test. Susceptibility on this variant, combined with rationality on the labeled SUNK_COST variant, would demonstrate that models can be trained away from sunk cost reasoning but do not generalize it spontaneously.

### Ecological validity and p2-05 strengths over Arkes & Blumer (1985)

BuyerBench's procurement framing offers several improvements in ecological validity:

1. **Organizational context:** CarrierA's account manager invokes the prior $72,000 in a realistic B2B sales conversation — the kind of vendor communication procurement managers routinely encounter. This is more realistic than the student-facing ski trip vignette.
2. **Ground-truth optimality:** Unlike the ski trip (where "enjoyability" is subjective), the carrier scoring formula is computable and unambiguous. CarrierB's 0.954 vs. CarrierA's 0.572 provides an uncontested normative benchmark for BSI calculation.
3. **Novel stimulus set:** The carrier/logistics context is unlikely to have appeared verbatim in LLM training data, reducing the training-data-contamination confound that would apply if the canonical ski trip vignette were used.
4. **Financial stake magnitude:** $72,000 in prior spend is within realistic organizational procurement budgets. The decision to continue with a carrier despite sunk costs has measurable 6-month cost implications ($24,000 in additional spending on CarrierA vs. the cheaper CarrierB alternative).

## 5. Paper Framing Guidance

When citing this paper in the BuyerBench manuscript:

- **Introduction/motivation:** Use Arkes & Blumer (1985) as the canonical demonstration that even economically consequential decisions (investment escalation, institutional contracts) are subject to sunk cost distortion in human populations. Establish that ~54% of human participants in the ski trip paradigm violate normative economic theory — setting a human baseline against which LLM behavior can be compared.

- **Related work:** Pair with Staw (1981) on escalation of commitment in organizational settings (confirms the effect in professional contexts) and Thaler (1980) on mental accounting (provides the theoretical mechanism: prior expenditure is coded in a "project account" that must be closed or justified). Note that Arkes & Blumer (1985) Study 4 (subsidy = $0 → no effect) provides the cleanest boundary condition and mechanism evidence.

- **Methodology:** Cite Arkes & Blumer (1985) for the sunk cost manipulation structure: irreversible prior expenditure + account manager framing as waste-generation creates the canonical sunk cost condition. Note that BuyerBench's p2-05 operationalizes this in a logistics carrier selection context with explicit weighted scoring, preserving the core irreversibility structure while extending to a professional domain with ground-truth optimality.

- **Results:**
  - **BSI ≈ 0.0 across all models (current observed pattern):** Frame as "LLMs with explicit evaluation rubrics and forward-looking constraints show no detectable sunk cost susceptibility at N=1 per cell. This contrasts with human performance (~54% susceptibility in the Arkes & Blumer paradigm) and suggests that explicit normative anchoring may eliminate sunk cost bias in language model agents." Hedge appropriately: single-run results require N ≥ 30 replication to distinguish zero-susceptibility from single-run noise.
  - **LLaMA 3.3 70B execution failure:** Classify separately as an operational robustness failure (fails both BASELINE and SUNK_COST), not a sunk cost susceptibility finding.
  - **If N ≥ 30 replication confirms BSI ≈ 0.0:** This is a substantive finding — the explicit rubric + labeled constraint appears to eliminate sunk cost susceptibility. Contrast with the p2-02 framing scenario (where framing also produces BSI ≈ 0.0) to build the pattern: *"LLMs are not susceptible to behavioral biases when given explicit quantitative scoring rubrics and normative constraints — but susceptibility may emerge in unstructured or high-ambiguity procurement scenarios."*

- **Limitations:** Acknowledge that (1) the explicit "past expenditures are sunk costs" constraint in the prompt may suppress susceptibility independent of genuine forward-looking reasoning; (2) single-run N=1 per model is insufficient to estimate stochastic bias rates; (3) absence of a `p2-05b` unlabeled variant prevents distinguishing constraint-following from genuine economic rationality.

- **Future work:** Propose a `p2-05b` variant removing the explicit sunk cost constraint to test whether susceptibility emerges without normative labeling. This would isolate whether the current null finding reflects: (a) genuine sunk cost immunity from explicit rubric prompting, or (b) LLMs recognizing and correcting for the fallacy when named but showing susceptibility when it is unlabeled.
