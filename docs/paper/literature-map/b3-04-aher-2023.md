# B3.04 — Using LLMs to Simulate Multiple Humans: Aher, Arriaga & Kalai (2023)

**Full citation:** Aher, G., Arriaga, R. I., & Kalai, A. T. (2023). Using Large Language Models to Simulate Multiple Humans and Replicate Human Subject Studies. In *Proceedings of the 40th International Conference on Machine Learning* (ICML 2023). PMLR 202:337–371.

**BibTeX key:** `aher2023simulating`

---

## 1. Empirical Design

Aher et al. (2023) introduce the **"Turing Experiment"** (TE) framework: a methodology for using LLMs as synthetic human respondents in classic social science experiments, with the explicit goal of assessing whether LLM response distributions can replicate observed human behavioral distributions. The paper's central empirical claim is that LLMs — particularly GPT-3 — can serve as synthetic subject pools that reproduce canonical findings from experimental economics and psychology at population-level accuracy.

### 1a. Experimental Paradigms Tested

The paper administers five classic human-subject paradigms to LLMs and compares the resulting response distributions to published human data:

| Experiment | Human source / canonical finding | Domain |
|---|---|---|
| **Ultimatum Game** | Güth et al. (1982); ~50% rejection rate for offers ≤ 20% | Economic fairness / game theory |
| **Dictator Game** | Forsythe et al. (1994); modal offer ≈ 20–30% of endowment | Economic altruism / social preferences |
| **Prisoner's Dilemma** | Axelrod (1984); cooperation rates 40–60% in one-shot | Strategic interdependence |
| **Wisdom of Crowds** | Galton (1907); aggregate estimate converges near true value | Judgment aggregation |
| **Garden Path Sentences** | Mitchell & Lapata (2008); reading time / interpretation errors | Cognitive linguistics |

For the Ultimatum Game — the most extensively analyzed paradigm — the authors present LLMs with the role of both Proposer and Responder across a sweep of offer amounts (5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, 25%, 30%, 50%). LLM "rejection rates" as Responder are then compared against the canonical human distribution showing approximately 50% rejection at the 20% offer level.

### 1b. Experimental Protocol

- **Models tested:** GPT-3 (text-davinci-002, text-davinci-003), GPT-3.5 Turbo, and several smaller GPT-3 variants (ada, babbage, curie). GPT-4 is not included (paper predates broad GPT-4 API access).
- **Persona conditioning:** A key methodological innovation is that each synthetic "respondent" is given a minimal persona description (age, occupation, educational background) sampled from US Census demographic distributions. This allows the paper to test whether LLMs can reproduce *demographic variation* in behavioral outcomes, not just mean behavior.
- **Sample size:** N ≈ 100 synthetic respondents per condition, generated by re-prompting with different persona seeds. This is the paper's answer to the stochasticity problem: rather than treating multiple runs as variance to be averaged, they treat them as a simulated subject pool with demographic heterogeneity.
- **Temperature:** Not systematically varied; a fixed sampling temperature is used across all simulated respondents.
- **Comparison baseline:** Published human data from the original experiments; no fresh human data collection.

### 1c. Key Findings

1. **Ultimatum Game:** LLMs (text-davinci-003) replicate the qualitative shape of the human rejection-rate curve — near-zero rejection at 50%, rising sharply as offers approach 10–15%. The modal rejection threshold aligns within ±5 percentage points of published human findings.
2. **Dictator Game:** LLM offers cluster around 20–30% of the endowment, consistent with the modal human offer distribution. GPT-3 models show somewhat higher altruism (offers around 30%) than humans.
3. **Demographic conditioning:** When personas are varied across gender, age, and education level, LLM response distributions show variation patterns that are directionally consistent with known demographic differences in human altruism and fairness norms. This is the paper's most novel finding: LLMs appear to have internalized *statistical correlates* of demographic subgroup behavior from training data.
4. **Prisoner's Dilemma:** Cooperation rates are broadly consistent with human one-shot PD rates, though LLMs show higher cooperation than humans in anonymous settings — the authors attribute this to LLMs' strong cooperative framing in instruction-following training.
5. **Wisdom of Crowds:** The aggregate of LLM estimates converges closer to the true value than individual estimates, replicating the crowd-averaging phenomenon — though this may partly reflect regression-to-mean in training-data aggregation rather than genuine wisdom-of-crowds dynamics.

## 2. Strengths

1. **Introduces a theoretically coherent framework (the Turing Experiment) for LLM-as-synthetic-respondent research.** The TE framework is not merely an empirical contribution but a methodological proposal that has become the standard reference point for all subsequent work using LLMs as synthetic subject pools. For BuyerBench, the TE framework provides a direct ancestor for our use of LLMs as AI buyer agents whose behavior is compared against normative benchmarks.

2. **Demographic persona conditioning is a genuine methodological advance.** By treating different LLM runs as synthetic respondents with heterogeneous demographic profiles rather than as variance around a single point estimate, Aher et al. transform the stochasticity problem (multiple runs produce different answers) into a feature (multiple runs simulate population heterogeneity). This is a creative reframing that BuyerBench can cite when defending its N=30 multi-run design as population-level inference rather than point estimation.

3. **Tests a battery of classic economic games — most relevant prior work to BuyerBench.** The Ultimatum Game and Dictator Game are foundational economic paradigms. Aher et al.'s demonstration that LLMs can reproduce human response distributions in these games establishes the baseline expectation that LLMs are not behaviorally random — they have structured preferences that align with human economic behavior in some domains. This motivates BuyerBench's hypothesis that LLMs will also exhibit human-like *biases* in procurement decisions.

4. **ICML publication establishes cross-community credibility.** A machine learning venue (ICML) publishing a social science methodology paper signals that the LLM-as-synthetic-subject approach has cross-disciplinary legitimacy. This supports BuyerBench's positioning at the intersection of AI/ML evaluation and behavioral economics.

5. **Population-level validation approach — not single-instance.** By comparing LLM response *distributions* to human response distributions (using KL divergence and distributional overlap metrics), the paper avoids the single-run point-estimate trap that plagues Binz & Schulz (2023) and early Hagendorff et al. results. BuyerBench's BSI is also a distribution-level metric (over N=30 runs per cell), and Aher et al. provides methodological precedent for this approach.

---

## 3. Limitations

1. **The stochastic parroting concern is the central methodological vulnerability.** The most significant critique of Aher et al. is articulated by Bender et al. (2021) as "stochastic parroting": large language models trained on vast text corpora may reproduce human behavioral patterns simply because those patterns are extensively documented in training data, not because the model has internalized any genuine decision-making process. In the specific case of Aher et al., the critique is particularly acute:
   - The Ultimatum Game is one of the most frequently discussed economic experiments in both academic and popular text. The fact that humans reject low offers because of fairness norms is described in hundreds of economics textbooks, review articles, and popularizations that are almost certainly in GPT-3's training corpus.
   - A model that has been trained on text saying "humans reject offers below 20% in the Ultimatum Game" will produce output that mimics this distribution when prompted as a human Ultimatum Game respondent — regardless of whether it has anything like a genuine fairness preference.
   - This confound is structurally unfalsifiable within Aher et al.'s design: the paper cannot distinguish "LLM reproduces human UG behavior because it has human-like fairness preferences" from "LLM reproduces human UG behavior because it was trained on text describing human UG behavior."

2. **No ground-truth optimal answer exists in the tested paradigms.** Aher et al. measure whether LLMs *replicate human behavior*, not whether LLMs make *economically rational decisions*. The Ultimatum Game Responder's equilibrium strategy (accept any positive offer) is known, but human rejection of low offers is the documented finding, not a bias deviation from optimum. BuyerBench's design — where an economically optimal choice can be computed for each scenario — addresses this directly by measuring deviation from rational optimum rather than deviation from observed human behavior.

3. **Single fixed temperature; no systematic stochasticity modeling.** The persona-conditioning approach ingeniously reframes between-run variance as demographic heterogeneity, but this requires the underlying model to be genuinely responsive to persona conditioning rather than producing approximately the same output regardless of persona. The paper does not independently verify that persona conditioning drives the observed between-respondent variance rather than temperature-induced randomness.

4. **No controlled-variant bias isolation.** Like Binz & Schulz and Hagendorff et al., Aher et al. do not administer a manipulated-cue variant alongside an unmanipulated baseline. Without BASELINE vs. BIAS_VARIANT controlled pairs, it is impossible to attribute behavioral differences to the specific manipulation of interest rather than to general task characteristics.

5. **Models tested (GPT-3 family) are substantially outdated relative to current frontier models.** The behavioral properties of text-davinci-002 and text-davinci-003 do not generalize to GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, or Gemini 1.5 Pro. The demographic conditioning results — which depend on the model's internalized representation of demographic-group behavioral correlates — may be substantially different, or reversed, in RLHF-aligned frontier models that have been trained to avoid demographic stereotyping.

6. **Ecological validity is limited to abstract economic games.** Ultimatum and Dictator Games are minimalist one-shot economic interactions with no contextual richness, no multi-step workflow, no domain expertise required, and no consequential external stakes. They are maximally abstract by design. The procurement decisions in BuyerBench involve multiple suppliers, complex cost structures, compliance requirements, and multi-step agent workflows — a substantially higher-fidelity economic environment.

## 4. Relevance to BuyerBench

### BuyerBench as a Domain-Specific Extension of the Aher et al. TE Framework

| Aher et al. limitation | BuyerBench response |
|---|---|
| Classic paradigms (UG, DG) with training-data-contamination risk | Novel procurement stimuli with low overlap with any documented experimental paradigm |
| No ground-truth economic optimality | Computable optimal supplier choice; BSI normalized against rational optimum |
| Simulation of human social preferences | Evaluation of AI agent decision quality against economic rationality standard |
| Replication of human distributions as the success criterion | Resistance to bias (deviation from optimum) as the success criterion |
| No controlled-variant bias isolation | Between-subject controlled-variant design (BASELINE vs. bias variant) |
| GPT-3 family only | Multi-model comparison across current frontier (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5/3.7, Gemini 1.5, LLaMA 3.x) |

### The Stochastic Parroting Critique — and BuyerBench's Structural Defense

The stochastic parroting critique (Bender et al., 2021) is the most important methodological challenge that BuyerBench must address, particularly in the framing effects (p2-02) and sunk cost (p2-05) scenarios where canonical paradigm descriptions circulate in LLM training corpora. BuyerBench has **three structural defenses** that should be articulated explicitly in the methodology section:

1. **Novel stimulus design.** BuyerBench scenarios are constructed from procurement-specific contexts — supplier catalogs, contract pricing structures, Q2 allocation windows — that do not map onto any documented cognitive psychology paradigm. No training corpus is likely to contain descriptions of "the correct response to the BuyerBench p2-01 anchoring scenario." This is a stronger stochastic-parroting defense than Aher et al. could offer for the Ultimatum Game.

2. **Ground-truth computability.** For Aher et al., the success criterion is reproducing human distributions — a criterion that is satisfied by training-data reproduction. For BuyerBench, the success criterion is selecting the economically optimal supplier — which requires genuine multi-attribute computation (price × volume + shipping cost + delay penalty), not reproduction of a known behavioral pattern. A model that blindly reproduces "human behavior" in a procurement context would be scored as biased (BSI > 0) whenever that behavior deviates from the optimum.

3. **Between-subject controlled-variant design eliminates the "familiar task = familiar answer" confound.** Stochastic parroting predicts that a model given the Asian Disease problem will reproduce the canonical 50/50 gain-frame vs. 70/30 loss-frame response pattern because that pattern is in its training data. But BuyerBench's controlled design asks: does the model's *decision change* when a procurement scenario is presented with a gain frame vs. a loss frame? If the underlying stimulus is novel (not in training data), training-data reproduction cannot explain frame-conditioned decision switching.

### The Persona Conditioning Insight — Applicable to BuyerBench's Multi-Run Design

Aher et al.'s most methodologically transferable contribution is the reconceptualization of multi-run LLM outputs as a synthetic respondent *distribution* rather than a single point estimate with noise. BuyerBench's N=30-per-cell design already implements this implicitly:

- Each of the 30 independent runs represents one draw from the LLM's conditional distribution over decisions given the scenario.
- The BSI is computed as the mean deviation from the optimum over those 30 draws — a distribution-level statistic.
- The within-cell variance (temperature noise) provides the denominator for hypothesis testing, exactly analogous to within-subject variance in human studies.

Aher et al. provides the citation precedent for framing this multi-run approach as a methodologically principled "simulation of stochastic agents" rather than a pragmatic workaround for LLM non-determinism.

In the BuyerBench methods section:

> "Following Aher et al. (2023), we treat each independent LLM run as a synthetic agent draw from the model's behavioral distribution, rather than as a noisy point estimate of a deterministic agent. The N=30 per-cell design yields a distributional BSI estimate with quantifiable sampling uncertainty."

### The Economic Game Precedent for Procurement Evaluation

Although Aher et al. focus on social preference games rather than procurement decisions, their demonstration that LLMs have structured, non-random economic preferences establishes the foundational premise that motivates BuyerBench: **LLMs are not behaviorally neutral**. If they were, there would be no point in testing for systematic biases. The Aher et al. results — that LLMs show fairness-consistent rejection patterns in the Ultimatum Game and altruism-consistent offer patterns in the Dictator Game — provide the empirical grounding for the claim that LLMs have economically structured preferences that can deviate systematically from optimality under manipulated cues.

BuyerBench's contribution is to shift the question from "do LLMs exhibit human-like social preferences?" (Aher et al.) to "do LLMs' structured economic preferences deviate systematically from rationality when subjected to behavioral manipulations?" — a question with direct relevance to the deployment of AI buyer agents in commercial procurement.

## 5. Paper Framing Guidance

When citing this paper in the BuyerBench manuscript:

- **Introduction:** Use Aher et al. to establish that prior work has demonstrated LLMs can replicate human behavioral distributions in economic games — and then immediately pivot: "However, replication of human behavior is not the appropriate criterion for evaluating AI procurement agents. The relevant benchmark is *economic rationality*, not human-likeness: an AI buyer agent should make the optimal procurement decision, not the decision a human would make. BuyerBench addresses this gap by measuring deviation from rational optimum (BSI) rather than deviation from human distributions."

- **Related Work:** Cite Aher et al. as the primary reference for the "LLMs as synthetic subjects" approach, alongside Binz & Schulz and Hagendorff et al. Note that Aher et al. is the ICML contribution most concerned with population-level behavioral distributions — making it the methodological ancestor for BuyerBench's N=30 multi-run design. Distinguish BuyerBench's use of LLMs as *evaluated agents* (not synthetic respondents): BuyerBench does not ask "do LLMs behave like humans?" but "do LLMs behave like rational buyers?"

- **Methodology:** Cite Aher et al.'s persona-conditioning as prior art for reconceptualizing LLM multi-run variance as distributional inference rather than point estimation. Explicitly invoke the stochastic parroting critique and document the three BuyerBench structural defenses above.

- **Discussion:** If BuyerBench finds near-zero BSI on paradigms that are heavily discussed in training data (framing, sunk cost), cite the stochastic parroting concern and note that novel stimuli cannot fully eliminate it — but that the controlled-variant design and ground-truth computability substantially reduce its scope. If BuyerBench finds positive BSI on low-contamination paradigms (scarcity, decoy), this is the strongest evidence against the stochastic parroting explanation.

- **Limitations:** Acknowledge that BuyerBench cannot fully rule out stochastic parroting for individual scenarios, and propose the unlabeled-variant upgrade (`p2-01b`, `p2-05b`) as the cleanest mitigation strategy.
